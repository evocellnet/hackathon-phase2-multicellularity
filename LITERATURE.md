# LITERATURE.md — Genomic transitions to multicellularity across the tree of life

*Literature review for the phase-2 multicellularity project (Ancestra-based ancestral gene content). Written 2026-10-28 by a literature-scientist agent.*

**How references were checked.** Every reference in the list at the end was looked up by tool, not from memory: PubMed E-utilities (esearch/esummary/efetch, via the PubMed connector and the script `agents/literature/verify_refs.py`), Crossref for DOIs not indexed in PubMed, and bioRxiv/web pages for preprints. The raw lookup log is `agents/literature/verification_log_raw.txt`. Title, first author, year, journal and DOI in the list are copied from the tool output. Claims about a paper's *findings* are taken from its abstract (fetched with `agents/literature/abstracts.py`) unless marked otherwise. Anything not checked this way is marked **[unverified]**. Preprints are marked **(preprint, not peer reviewed)**.

**Main points for the design**

1. **Origins.** Multicellularity evolved many times. A recent critical census counts **45 independent multicellular eukaryotic lineages of six types**, and estimates the true number is "likely close to a hundred" (Lamża 2023). *Complex* multicellularity is restricted to 5–6 eukaryotic clades: animals, land plants, florideophyte red algae, brown algae, and fungi (Knoll 2011 counts two fungal groups; Nagy et al. 2018 count 8–11 convergent origins inside fungi). Several bacterial lineages are multicellular, and archaeal multicellularity is rare (Claessen et al. 2014; Rados et al. 2025).
2. **Genomic consensus so far.** Most "multicellularity toolkit" genes predate each transition (co-option). On top of this, there are lineage-specific gains, expansions of transcription-factor (TF), signalling and adhesion/extracellular-matrix (ECM) families, and regulatory rewiring. Whether the transition nodes carry *unusually large* bursts of novelty is disputed: Paps & Holland 2018, Bowles et al. 2020 and Denoeud et al. 2024 report bursts; Prochnik et al. 2010, Glöckner et al. 2016 and Kiss et al. 2019 report limited novelty. Estimates of gene gain depend strongly on how densely the outgroups are sampled (Richter et al. 2018). A study controlled across lineages and using one pipeline for all independent transitions, including aggregative and prokaryotic ones, has not been done. Pereira Lobo et al. 2025 is the closest, but covers complex lineages only. This is the gap Ancestra can fill.
3. **Selection pressure.** Theory predicts that multicellularity lowers the effective population size (Ne) and so strengthens genetic drift (Lynch & Conery 2003; Bingham & Ratcliff 2024). Experiments support this (Pentz et al. 2023). We found no systematic genome-wide test of relaxed selection that spans the independent transitions. Deep divergences saturate synonymous sites, so codon dN/dS is usable only for young transitions. Amino-acid relative-rate methods (RERconverge), protein length, intrinsic disorder and domain architecture are the realistic signals at deep nodes.

## 1. Definitions and typology of multicellularity

**Main point:** "multicellularity" is not one trait. It is a set of separable properties: how the group forms, whether the organism can live without it, how many cell types it has, and whether cell compartments are physically complete. Our trait coding must keep these properties apart rather than collapse them into a single yes/no.

### 1.1 Core axes

**Clonal vs aggregative development.** In *clonal* multicellularity, daughter cells stay together after division, so the group passes through a single-cell bottleneck and its cells are genetically identical. In *aggregative* multicellularity, free-living cells, which can differ in genotype, come together (Grosberg & Strathmann 2007; Brunet & King 2017). Grosberg & Strathmann (2007) argue that development from a single cell is the most important defence against conflict between cell lineages. Within-organism competition may explain why organisms that develop by aggregation generally show less cell differentiation. A comparative analysis by Fisher, Cornwallis & West (2013) supports this: species with clonal group formation were more likely to have become obligately multicellular, had more cell types, and were more likely to have sterile cells. Márquez-Zacarías et al. (2021) note that **all known aggregative multicellular organisms are facultatively multicellular**. They argue that a mostly unicellular life cycle, and not only social conflict, may limit how complex aggregative lineages become. Pentz et al. (2020) engineered snowflake (clonal) and floc (aggregative) yeast and found that clonal groups exploited aggregative ones. Day et al. (2022) classify intercellular bonds as reformable or non-reformable and argue that this early choice constrains later evolution.

**Simple vs complex.** Knoll (2011) defines *complex* multicellularity as more than adhesion: it requires cell-to-cell channels for moving nutrients and signals, and differentiated tissues. He restricts it to "animals, embryophytic land plants, florideophyte red algae, laminarialean brown algae, and two groups of fungi". Nagy, Kovács & Krizsán (2018) instead find **8–11 convergent origins** of complex multicellularity in fungi (fruiting bodies). The number of cell types is the usual quantitative proxy for complexity (Bell & Mooers 1997; Vogel & Chothia 2006; Schad et al. 2011; Pereira Lobo et al. 2025).

**Facultative vs obligate.** Some organisms live as single cells and form groups only under certain conditions. Examples: *Salpingoeca rosetta* rosettes, induced by a bacterial sulfonolipid (Alegado et al. 2012; Dayel et al. 2011); the aggregative stage of *Capsaspora* (Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2013); dictyostelid fruiting under starvation (Du et al. 2015). Others are multicellular throughout their life cycle (animals, *Volvox*). Fisher et al. (2013) treat obligate multicellularity as the true "major transition" in the sense of Szathmáry & Maynard Smith (1995). Queller & Strassmann (2009) frame the same question as a continuum of "organismality", defined by high cooperation and low conflict among parts.

### 1.2 Grey zones

- **Incomplete cytokinesis and cytoplasmic bridges.** Chaigne & Brunet (2022) review cytoplasmic bridges, usually formed by incomplete abscission (the final cut that separates two daughter cells). They find bridges widespread, "and likely ancestrally present, in almost all lineages of eukaryotes with clonal multicellularity — including the five complex multicellular lineages". *S. rosetta* rosette cells stay connected by intercellular bridges, and all four septins are up-regulated in colonies (Fairclough et al. 2013).
- **Coenocytes and syncytia.** A coenocyte is a cell with many nuclei. Ichthyosporeans grow as coenocytes and then cellularize: in *Sphaeroforma arctica* the timing is set by the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and the process forms a polarized layer of cells (Ondracka et al. 2018; Dudin et al. 2019; Olivetta & Dudin 2023). In *Chromosphaera perkinsii*, single cells divide by cleavage into a long-lived colony with distinct co-existing cell types (Olivetta et al. 2024). Other examples are the plasmodia of myxomycetes such as *Physarum* (Schaap et al. 2015), siphonous green seaweeds (Del Cortona et al. 2020), and the non-septate hyphae of early-diverging fungi and oomycetes. Lamża (2023) counts **septate multinucleate thalli** (Chytridiomycota, Blastocladiomycota, Pseudofungi) as clonal multicellularity.
- **Colonies and filaments.** Volvocine colonies range from 4-celled *Tetrabaena* to *Volvox* with germ–soma differentiation (Kirk 2005; Umen & Herron 2021). Other examples are filamentous cyanobacteria (Herrero et al. 2016) and longitudinally dividing multicellular filaments of the Neisseriaceae (*Alysiella*, *Simonsiella*; Nyongesa et al. 2022). In each case, whether to call the organism "multicellular" depends on integration criteria such as a shared life cycle, communication between cells and division of labour.
- **Lamża's six types** (Lamża 2023): (i) septate multinucleate thalli, (ii) multicellular reproductive structures formed by plasmotomy (division of a multinucleate cell; e.g. Phytomyxea), (iii) endogenous budding (Paramyxida), (iv) non-separation of cells after division (Metazoa, Ochrophyta), (v) pseudoplasmodial aggregation (sorocarps such as Acrasida), and (vi) meroplasmodial aggregation (e.g. Variosea, *Filoreta*). A possible seventh type is multi-species "superorganisms" of protists with obligate bacterial symbionts.
- **Experimental de novo multicellularity.** Simple clonal multicellularity evolves readily in the laboratory. Examples: snowflake yeast (Ratcliff et al. 2012, 2015); macroscopic snowflake yeast after 600 rounds of selection (Bozdag et al. 2023), with whole-genome duplication to tetraploidy (Tong et al. 2025); predator-induced multicellularity in *Chlamydomonas* (Herron et al. 2019), which depends on the founder genotype (Chen et al. 2025). The genomic "signature" of a *nascent* transition may therefore be small.

### 1.3 Hypotheses on the origin of differentiation

Brunet & King (2017) propose two routes to animal cell types: division of labour among ancient multifunctional cells, and conversion of phenotypes that alternated in time into cell types side by side in space. The second route follows Mikhailov et al. (2009). Nedelcu and colleagues propose that cell types arise when stress-response genes are co-opted. In *Volvox*, the somatic master regulator *regA* derives from a stress-induced *RLS1*-like gene (Nedelcu & Michod 2020; König & Nedelcu 2020). Niklas & Newman (2013) describe a morphological series running from unicellular through colonial and filamentous to parenchymatous forms (parenchyma: tissue built by cell division in all planes).

**Implication for our coding.** For each node we should record four things: (a) development mode, C (clonal) or A (aggregative); (b) facultative vs obligate; (c) simple vs complex, using a cell-type count where available (Fisher et al. 2013; Pereira Lobo et al. 2025); and (d) a grey-zone flag for coenocytic, syncytial, colonial or filamentous forms. Each test should then be run under a strict coding and a permissive coding.

## 2. Catalogue of independent origins of multicellularity

**Main point:** the transitions for which genome data exist on *both* sides of the node, with several outgroups, are few. They are Metazoa, Fungi (hyphae, complex fruiting bodies, and yeast reversals), Dictyostelia, volvocine algae, streptophytes, red algae, brown algae, *Ulva*/Ulvophyceae, and among prokaryotes cyanobacteria, Myxococcota and *Streptomyces*. Most aggregative origins are each represented by a single genome or transcriptome. Some have no proteome at all.

The **approximate number of origins** depends on the definition. Lamża (2023) counts 45 multicellular eukaryotic lineages and estimates about 100 in total. "At least 25" origins across life is often quoted from Grosberg & Strathmann (2007) **[unverified: the abstract we checked gives no number]**. Parfrey & Lahr (2013) stress "numerous other origins … including three within Amorphea" beyond animals, fungi and dictyostelids, and argue against a single origin of multicellularity in Amorphea. Complex multicellularity: 5 or 6 eukaryotic clades (Knoll 2011; Bingham & Ratcliff 2024), with 8–11 origins inside fungi (Nagy et al. 2018). Bacteria: "several" independent origins (Claessen et al. 2014; Lyons & Kolter 2015). Archaea: before 2025 only one case was considered established (Rados et al. 2025).

**Notes on the table.**
- Within volvocines, Lindsey et al. (2021) find "multicellularity independently evolved at least twice" and cellular differentiation "at least four, and possibly as many as six times". This makes the volvocines a natural replicated system.
- Ages are lineage ages taken from dating papers and carry large uncertainty (dos Reis et al. 2015 show how wide the bounds are for animals).
- For cyanobacteria, Yang et al. (2026) and Hammerschmidt et al. (2021) report repeated gains *and losses* of multicellular traits. Simple "one origin" coding is therefore inadequate.

## 3. Prior genomic findings on the transitions

**Main point:** across lineages, the consensus is that the transition **co-opted an older repertoire**. Many adhesion, signalling and TF families are present in the unicellular relatives. Some lineage-specific genes were gained: mostly extracellular, sensing and ECM proteins. TF and signalling families expanded, and gene regulation changed. Two questions remain unsettled: whether transition nodes are *unusual* compared with other nodes, and how much of the reported gain is an artefact of sampling.

### 3.1 Animals (the best-studied case)

- **Toolkit before animals.** The genome of *Monosiga brevicollis* contains cadherins, tyrosine kinases and other "animal" genes (King et al. 2008; Abedin & King 2008; Manning et al. 2008). *Capsaspora* has integrin adhesion machinery (Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2010), many animal TFs (Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2011), the Hippo pathway (Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2012), and "a richer repertoire of proteins involved in cell adhesion and transcriptional regulation" than choanoflagellates. However, "most intercellular signalling systems controlling development evolved later concomitant with the emergence of the first metazoans" (Suga et al. 2013). Receptor tyrosine kinases radiated several times independently (Suga et al. 2012). Notch and Delta, and homologues of Toll-like receptors, predate the split between animals and choanoflagellates (Richter et al. 2018; see also Gazave et al. 2009).
- **Ancient burst at Holozoa.** Grau-Bové et al. (2017) find "an early burst of gene diversity in the ancestor of Holozoa, enriched in transcription factors and cell adhesion machinery". Later episodes of synteny disruption, intron gain and genome expansion followed *before* animals appeared.
- **Novelty at the metazoan stem.** Richter et al. (2018) add 19 choanoflagellate transcriptomes. They infer ~1,944 gene families that originated on the animal stem, only 39 of which are conserved in all animals. ~372 families previously thought animal-specific turned out to predate the animal–choanoflagellate split. Paps & Holland (2018) report "an unprecedented increase in the extent of genomic novelty during the origin of metazoans", compared with other eukaryotic ancestors, and 25 metazoan-specific gene groups that are essential across animals. Ocaña-Pallarès et al. (2022) find that genes important for multicellularity accumulated gradually in pre-metazoan ancestors and the process accelerated at the metazoan root. Pre-fungal ancestors instead show net losses. Gene fusions are more frequent in Metazoa, and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is more frequent in fungi and protists.
- **Regulation.** *Capsaspora* has dynamic chromatin states and conserved TF networks but lacks animal promoter types and distal enhancers. Sebé-Pedrós et al. (2016) conclude that a "major shift in genome cis-regulatory complexity, most notably the appearance of distal enhancer regulation" accompanied animal origins. Regulated alternative splicing appears in the aggregative stage of *Capsaspora* (Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2013), and exon skipping became widespread in animals (Grau-Bové et al. 2018).
- **Losses.** Gene loss is widespread across animal evolution (Guijarro-Clarke et al. 2020; Fernández & Gabaldón 2020).
- Reviews: Rokas 2008a; Sebé-Pedrós, Degnan & Ruiz-Trillo 2017; Brunet & King 2017; Ros-Rocher et al. 2021; Ruiz-Trillo et al. 2023.

### 3.2 Fungi

Hyphae arose early in fungal evolution "via diverse genetic changes, including co-option and exaptation of ancient eukaryotic (e.g. phagocytosis-related) genes". In contrast to most multicellular lineages, their origin "did not correlate with expansions of kinases, receptors or adhesive proteins". Kiss et al. (2019) found 414 novel gene families whose evolution correlates with hyphae. Merényi et al. (2023) describe early fungal evolution as "gradual shedding of protist genes" punctuated by two main duplication events. The fastest-duplicating groups include extracellular proteins and TFs. Complex fruiting bodies arose convergently. In Agaricomycotina and Pezizomycotina, "very similar sets of genes have convergently been co-opted … followed by expansions of their gene families by duplications" (Merényi et al. 2020). The authors interpret this as an ancestral "predisposition". Krizsán et al. (2019) report a transcriptomic atlas of mushroom development. Gene age shapes transcription during fruiting-body development (Merényi et al. 2022). *Neolecta* shows that rudimentary complex multicellularity is deep in Ascomycota, and yeasts show parallel divergence in the same genes (Nguyen et al. 2017). Yeast forms arose repeatedly through parallel diversification of Zn-cluster TFs (Nagy et al. 2014). A recent study detected convergent amino-acid substitutions in the septal-pore-cap gene *spc33* (Iizuka et al. 2026). Overall, the fungal evidence points to regulation and co-option, with expansions limited to particular families.

### 3.3 Aggregative amoebae

**Dictyostelia.** "80% of proteins essential for the development of multicellular Dictyostelia are already present in their unicellular relatives". The remaining 20% that are unique to Dictyostelia are "mostly extracellularly exposed and secreted proteins, with roles in sensing and recognition". Some signal-synthesis genes came by HGT. Changes in gene expression track phenotypic innovation better than changes in protein domains. The authors conclude that the transition "required novel signals and sensors rather than novel signal processing mechanisms" (Glöckner et al. 2016). Genes for secondary metabolism, export and signal perception expanded differentially between dictyostelid groups (Heidel et al. 2011). See also Schilde et al. 2016, Hall et al. 2022 (G-protein-coupled receptors, GPCRs), and the reviews of Du et al. 2015 and Kin & Schaap 2021.

**Acrasis.** The *Acrasis kona* genome is "rich in novelty and genes with strong signatures of horizontal transfer". Development is "molecularly simple" compared with *Dictyostelium*, yet uses "remarkably similar pathways for signaling, motility and … extracellular matrix". The authors conclude that "much of the basic tool kit for multicellular development arose early in eukaryote evolution" (Sheikh et al. 2024).

**Other aggregative lineages.** *Fonticula*, *Copromyxa*, *Guttulinopsis*, *Sorodiplophrys* and *Sorogena* have no dedicated comparative-genomic study that we could find. Their papers are phylogenetic placements (Brown et al. 2009, 2011, 2012a; Tice et al. 2016; Lasek-Nesselquist & Katz 2001). This is a clear opportunity.

### 3.4 Green lineage

**Volvocine algae.** The *Volvox* proteome is similar in coding potential to that of *Chlamydomonas*, with few species-specific genes. It is enriched in volvocine-specific ECM proteins, and the authors conclude that complexity can come from "modifications of lineage-specific proteins rather than large-scale invention" (Prochnik et al. 2010). Colony formation in *Gonium* evolved through co-option of the retinoblastoma cell-cycle pathway; expressing the *Gonium* retinoblastoma regulator makes *Chlamydomonas* colonial (Hanschen et al. 2016). In *Tetrabaena*, a minimal set of gene families expanded at the origin of multicellularity, enriched for developmental processes. Genes under positive or accelerated selection in multicellular volvocines are enriched for the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (Featherston et al. 2018). The germ–soma regulator family *regA*/*RLS* is present in undifferentiated relatives (Grochau-Wright et al. 2017) and was co-opted from stress responses (König & Nedelcu 2020). In a review, Jiménez-Marín et al. (2022) find no "master multicellularity genes" and favour regulatory and functional overhauls of shared repertoires.

**Streptophytes and land plants.** Bowles et al. (2020) analysed 208 genomes and found two bursts of "fundamental genomic novelties": one at the origin of streptophytes (Ediacaran) and one at land plants (Ordovician). They argue the genomic origins of multicellularity and of terrestrialization are separate. The Zygnematophyceae/land-plant ancestor had "expanded genes for signaling cascades, environmental response, and multicellular growth" (Feng et al. 2024). *Chara* shows "secondary complexity" (Nishiyama et al. 2018). A unicellular/multicellular sister pair, *Interfilum*/*Klebsormidium*, is now sequenced (Ma et al. 2026). The number of TF families increased stepwise in the Precambrian (Catarino et al. 2016), and TF repertoires correlate with complexity (Lang et al. 2010). Plant receptor-like kinases form a family related to animal receptor kinases (Shiu & Bleecker 2001).

**Ulva.** In *Ulva*, the "gene family expansions associated with multicellularity are distinct from those of freshwater algae" (De Clerck et al. 2018). Green seaweeds acquired macroscopic growth several times (Del Cortona et al. 2020).

Reviews: Umen 2014; Umen & Herron 2021.

### 3.5 Brown and red algae

**Brown algae.** Multicellularity in *Ectocarpus* "is correlated with the presence of a rich array of signal transduction genes", in particular receptor kinases. The authors note that related molecules evolved independently in animals and plants (Cock et al. 2010). The multi-genome analysis of Denoeud et al. (2024) finds that the emergence of brown algae "was associated with a marked gain of new orthologous gene families, enhanced protein domain rearrangement, increased horizontal gene transfer events", along with new signalling molecules and alginate ECM pathways. Large viral genomes integrated throughout the lineage's history.

**Red algae.** Red algal genomes are often reduced (Collén et al. 2013; Brawley et al. 2017). *Bostrychia* (Ceramiales) instead shows genome expansion driven by giant *Plavaka* DNA transposons, de novo gene emergence and amplified families. Its sex chromosomes carry TALE-homeodomain TFs "orthologous to ancient regulators of haploid-diploid transitions in other multicellular lineages" (Petroll et al. 2025). Red algal complexity was gained secondarily, with TF signatures (Petroll et al. 2021). *Bangia* is "the most basal multicellular red algal lineage, diverging ∼973 Ma" (Hui et al. 2026). Reviews: Borg et al. 2023; Coelho & Weijers 2026 (convergence across land plants and brown and red algae).

### 3.6 Prokaryotes

**Cyanobacteria.** Hammerschmidt et al. (2021) reconstruct the order in which traits appeared: nitrogen fixation, then filamentous morphology, then a reproductive life cycle, then differentiated and patterned multicellularity. Yang et al. (2026) reconstruct ancestral gene content with gains and losses across morphological transitions. They find convergence in "signal transduction, cell division, and transcriptional control", and observe that reversals to unicellularity lose genes for "motility, regulatory complexity, and cytoskeletal organization". This is a useful methodological precedent for our approach.

**Myxobacteria.** The *Myxococcus* genome shows expanded sensory and signal-transduction systems (Goldman et al. 2006). The developmental programmes differ between myxobacterial suborders (Huntley et al. 2011). *M. xanthus* has a high mutation rate and a below-average Ne (~10^7) for bacteria (Wielgoss et al. 2024).

**Others.** *Streptomyces* antibiotic production is organised by division of labour through terminal genomic differentiation (Zhang et al. 2020). Candidate adhesion proteins of *Ca.* Magnetoglobus are homologous to proteins of unrelated filamentous multicellular bacteria (Abreu et al. 2014). In Neisseriaceae, multicellularity is associated with acquiring the amidase gene *amiC2* and with substitutions in MreB and FtsA (Nyongesa et al. 2022).

## 4. Selection pressure, evolutionary rate, and proteome-architecture theory and evidence

**Main point:** there is a clear population-genetic prediction. Multicellularity lowers Ne, which weakens purifying selection, which allows genome and protein complexity to grow. Some proteome-architecture correlates exist, but they mostly compare prokaryotes, eukaryotes and animals and are not resolved per transition. We found no study that tests relaxed selection *genome-wide and across independent transitions with matched unicellular controls*. Our project could provide one, as long as it uses methods that survive deep divergence.

### 4.1 Theory

Lynch & Conery (2003) argue that small Ne in multicellular eukaryotes allows slightly deleterious genomic complexity (introns, mobile elements, gene duplicates) to accumulate. Lynch (2007) calls adaptive explanations for organismal complexity "frail", and Lynch et al. (2016) link drift to the evolution of mutation rates. Bingham & Ratcliff (2024) extend this specifically to multicellularity. Binding cells into groups with finite bottlenecks between generations "greatly reduces the effective population size (Ne) of cellular populations". Eukaryotes respond to drift with genome expansion and prokaryotes with genome erosion, which could explain why complex multicellularity is restricted to eukaryotes. Experimental support: clonal snowflake yeast showed "much higher rates of genetic drift" than aggregative floc yeast (Pentz et al. 2023). *M. xanthus*, an aggregative bacterium, has an elevated mutation rate and below-average Ne (Wielgoss et al. 2024); in bacteria generally, genetic drift is associated with reduced genome complexity (Kuo et al. 2009).

### 4.2 Proteome-level correlates of complexity

- **Protein length and proteome size.** Eukaryotic proteins are longer than prokaryotic ones (Brocchieri & Karlin 2005). Complexity, measured as the number of cell types, correlates with total proteome size in amino acids, following a power law (Schad et al. 2011).
- **Intrinsic disorder.** The fraction of disordered residues "increases significantly between prokaryotes and eukaryotes but does not further increase over the course of evolution" (Schad et al. 2011). Across 3,500 proteomes, disorder is higher in eukaryotes (Xue et al. 2012). In TFs, the number of TFs, their splice variants and their total disordered residues correlate strongly with cell-type number (r² > 0.8 across 17 species; Yruela et al. 2017). See also Ahrens et al. 2017, Dunker et al. 2015 and Niklas et al. 2015. These are mostly cross-sectional correlations without phylogenetic replication. Yruela et al. themselves caution that correlation is not causation.
- **Alternative splicing.** After correcting for transcript coverage, splicing correlates strongly with cell-type number (Chen et al. 2014). Exon-skipping-rich transcriptomes arose in animals through changes in gene architecture (Grau-Bové et al. 2018).
- **Domain architecture.** Domain rearrangement rates are elevated in Metazoa (Ekman et al. 2007). Domain promiscuity (the tendency of a domain to combine with many partners) evolved within eukaryotes (Basu et al. 2008). Domain combinations differ across eukaryote lineages (Itoh et al. 2007; Ekman et al. 2005). Brown algal emergence shows "enhanced protein domain rearrangement" (Denoeud et al. 2024), and gene fusions are more prevalent in Metazoa (Ocaña-Pallarès et al. 2022).
- **Gene family expansion and cell types.** Families that expanded in complex organisms are enriched in extracellular and regulatory functions (Vogel & Chothia 2006).

### 4.3 Direct evidence on rate or selection shifts at transitions

This evidence is sparse. Featherston et al. (2018) find genes under "positive or accelerating selection" in multicellular volvocines enriched for the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. Merényi et al. (2020) and Iizuka et al. (2026) find convergent co-option and convergent substitutions in fungi. A structure-based phylogeny of 1,542 UniProt proteomes reports "structural evolutionary acceleration" in some animal lineages (Li, Daumiller & Bryant 2026, preprint, not peer reviewed; summary from the full-text page). Searches of PubMed and the web for genome-wide dN/dS comparisons between multicellular lineages and their unicellular sisters across transitions returned no such study. We report this as **an absence in our searches, not a proof of absence**.

### 4.4 Methods to detect trait-associated rate shifts

| Method | What it tests | Data | Fit to our problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RERconverge** (Chikina et al. 2016; Kowalczyk et al. 2019) | Correlation between gene-specific relative rates (branch length relative to the genome-wide expectation) and a binary or continuous trait across independent origins | Amino-acid or nucleotide gene trees on a fixed topology | **Best fit**: works on protein trees at deep divergence; uses convergence across origins |
| **Forward Genomics** (Hiller et al. 2012; Prudent et al. 2016) | Sequence divergence associated with independent trait losses | Ancestral reconstructions, branch-wise | Useful for *reversals* (yeasts, unicellular cyanobacteria) |
| **PhyloAcc** (Hu et al. 2019) | Bayesian rate shifts in conserved elements on trait branches | Mostly noncoding elements | Concept is transferable; designed for noncoding DNA |
| **HyPhy RELAX** (Wertheim et al. 2015), **aBSREL** (Smith et al. 2015), HyPhy 2.5 (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020) | Relaxation or intensification of selection (k parameter) on test vs reference branches; episodic positive selection | Codon alignments | Only for **young** transitions (inside volvocines, *Ulva*, fungal fruiting bodies, cyanobacterial clades), because dS saturates at deep nodes (Gharib & Robinson-Rechavi 2013) |
| **TraitRELAX** (Halabi et al. 2021) | RELAX combined with a model of trait evolution | Codon data plus trait | Same constraint as RELAX |
| **CSUBST** (Fukushima & Pollock 2023), **PCOC** (Rey et al. 2018) | Convergent amino-acid substitutions on independent trait branches, with error correction | Codon/protein alignments with ancestral reconstruction | Directly suited to replicated transitions (e.g. adhesion or TF orthogroups shared across origins) |
| Phylogenetic logistic regression (Ives & Garland 2010); Pagel (1994) correlated evolution | Association between gene presence or copy number and a trait | Presence/absence or copy-number matrices | For HOG-by-trait association, as in the phylogeny-aware design of Pereira Lobo et al. 2025 |

**Structure-based approaches.** AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al. 2021), AlphaFold DB (>214 M models; Varadi et al. 2022, 2024) and ESMFold (Lin et al. 2023) provide models for most proteomes. Foldseek allows fast structural search (van Kempen et al. 2024) and has been used to cluster the AFDB (Barrio-Hernandez et al. 2023). FoldMason performs multiple structure alignment (Gilchrist et al. 2026). Structural phylogenetics is now feasible: Malik et al. 2020; FoldTree in Moi et al. 2025; and an overview by Puente-Lelievre et al. 2026. Rate variation among sites relates to structural constraints (Echave et al. 2016), and adaptive changes concentrate in particular structural contexts (Slodkowicz & Goldman 2020). Practical uses for us include remote homology detection, so that "novel" HOGs are not artefacts of sequence divergence; comparing structural divergence before and after transitions; and per-residue confidence (pLDDT) and disorder as traits. Disorder can be predicted at scale with AIUPred (Erdős & Dosztányi 2024) or metapredict (Emenecker et al. 2021).

**Caveats.** The two sides of a transition often differ in data type (transcriptomes vs genomes). They also differ in protein length and disorder, which inflates alignment-based rates. Rate methods therefore need trimmed, length-matched alignments, and controls for proteome completeness (Section 5).

## 5. Methods for ancestral gene content reconstruction and their pitfalls

**Main point:** ancestral gene content can be inferred by parsimony, likelihood or reconciliation. Systematic errors from annotation, contamination, homology detection and sampling dominate the results more than the choice of method. Every transition-node result must be compared with **matched control nodes** that have similar branch length and sampling.

### 5.1 Approaches

- **Dollo parsimony.** Each family is gained once and may be lost many times. This is conservative for gains, but a single spurious presence (contamination, HGT) pushes the gain deep into the tree. **Wagner parsimony** allows repeated gains. **Count** implements both, plus a phylogenetic birth–death likelihood with rate variation (Csűrös 2010; Csűrös & Miklós 2009). Other likelihood tools for copy number are **CAFE 5** (Mendes et al. 2021) and **BadiRate** (Librado et al. 2012).
- **Gene-tree/species-tree reconciliation.** ALE (Szöllősi et al. 2013, 2015), GeneRax (Morel et al. 2020) and AleRax (Morel et al. 2024) model duplication, transfer and loss explicitly. They are more accurate but expensive. Vosseberg et al. (2021) show how reconciled duplications can be used to time innovations.
- **Hierarchical orthologous groups (HOGs).** A HOG is the set of genes descended from a single ancestral gene at a given taxonomic level, so each HOG at a level is one inferred ancestral gene. Tools include OMA (Altenhoff et al. 2013, 2024), FastOMA (Majidian et al. 2025), Hieranoid (Schreiber & Sonnhammer 2013) and pyHam, which extracts gains, losses and duplications per branch (Train et al. 2019). The flat orthogroups of OrthoFinder (Emms & Kelly 2019) or Broccoli (Derelle et al. 2020) need a separate step to map them to ancestors. Orthology benchmarking is done through the Quest for Orthologs (Altenhoff et al. 2016).
- **Ancestra** (`external/ancestra-mvp-ai/README.md`). Ancestra reconstructs HOGs bottom-up, level by level along a guide species tree. It first clusters proteins into families without a reference database (DIAMOND), then merges child-lineage genes at each node by mutual best matches and in-paralog absorption. Ambiguous clusters are resolved with small local trees, and gene order can optionally be used. Its README lists bottom-up methods such as SYNERGY and Hieranoid, and OMA/FastOMA, as its predecessors. The output is OrthoXML that loads in pyHam. Relevant limitations stated in the README:
  - It expects one protein per gene; isoforms are not selected.
  - The optional structure "rescue" step lowered OrthoBench precision.
  - It uses NCBI-taxonomy guide trees.
  - Its benchmarks are on OrthoBench and QfO, which are dominated by animals and fungi. Behaviour on deep, protist-rich trees is untested.

### 5.2 Pitfalls and how earlier studies handled them

1. **Annotation completeness and false absences.** Incomplete gene predictions create spurious losses, and the effect is quantified in Deutekom et al. (2019). Transcriptomes miss genes that are not expressed and carry fragments. Completeness can be assessed with BUSCO (Manni et al. 2021) or OMArk, which also reports contamination and inconsistent annotations (Nevers et al. 2025). EukProt v3 has 497 transcriptomes, 375 genomes and 103 single-cell datasets among its 993 species (our count). It provides "The Comparative Set" of 196 high-completeness proteomes (Richter et al. 2022).
2. **Contamination.** Contamination produces false "gains" deep in the tree and false HGT. Tools: Conterminator (Steinegger & Salzberg 2020), CroCo for cross-contamination between transcriptomes (Simion et al. 2018), BlobToolKit (Challis et al. 2020), EukCC (Saary et al. 2020). A 2026 screen of the P10K protist genomes detected contamination (Porfirio-Sousa et al. 2026).
3. **Homology detection failure.** On long branches, fast-evolving genes look "novel". Gene-age (phylostratigraphy) results are debated: bias was reported by Moyers & Zhang (2015) and disputed by Domazet-Lošo et al. (2017). Weisman et al. (2020) show that "many, but not all" lineage-specific genes can be explained by detection failure. Consensus gene ages are discussed by Liebeskind et al. (2016). Structure-aware searches (Foldseek) partly mitigate this.
4. **Outgroup sampling density.** Adding 19 choanoflagellate transcriptomes moved ~372 "animal-specific" families to before the animal–choanoflagellate split (Richter et al. 2018). Poorly sampled outgroups therefore **inflate gains** on the stem branch. The problem is worst for single-taxon aggregative lineages (*Fonticula*, *Copromyxa*, *Guttulinopsis*), where the "transition branch" is also the terminal branch of one species.
5. **Long-branch attraction (LBA) and tree uncertainty.** A wrong guide tree misplaces gains and losses (Bergsten 2005; Philippe et al. 2011). The position of sponges vs ctenophores at the animal root is a well-known example (Simion et al. 2017; Laumer et al. 2019; Schultz et al. 2023). For eukaryote-wide trees, Burki et al. (2020) and Leroy et al. (2026, preprint) summarise the current topology and its uncertainties.
6. **HGT.** Under Dollo parsimony, an HGT event looks like a deep gain plus many losses. HGT is frequent in fungi and protists (Ocaña-Pallarès et al. 2022; Szöllősi et al. 2015), brown algae (Denoeud et al. 2024), *Acrasis* (Sheikh et al. 2024) and dictyostelid signal synthesis (Glöckner et al. 2016).
7. **Control nodes.** Paps & Holland (2018) compared the novelty at the metazoan node with that of other eukaryotic ancestors. Bowles et al. (2020) did the same across plant nodes. Ocaña-Pallarès et al. (2022) contrasted the animal and fungal trajectories. Kiss et al. (2019) tested correlated evolution between gene families and hyphae across the tree rather than at one node. Yang et al. (2026) compared gains with losses at reversals.

**Recommendations for us:**
- Normalise gains per unit branch length and per number of descendant proteomes.
- Compare each transition branch with a distribution of non-transition branches of similar length and sampling.
- Run rarefaction: drop outgroup taxa and see how gain estimates move.
- Use reversals as converse tests.

| **H16** | **Regulatory rather than coding change dominates** (only partly testable with proteomes) | Sebé-Pedrós et al. 2016; Jiménez-Marín et al. 2022; Nagy et al. 2014 | Expected *negative* result for H2 in volvocines and fungal hyphae, together with TF expansions. **Strongest, most data-ready hypotheses:** H1/H2 (as one comparison), H4, H5, H8, H9 and H12. They need only proteomes, Pfam annotation, HOG gains, losses and duplications from Ancestra, and per-node branch lengths. All have clearly opposed predictions from named authors.
